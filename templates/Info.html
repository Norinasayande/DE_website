What is a Food System?

The food system is a complex web of activities involving the production, processing, transport, and consumption. Issues concerning the food system include the governance and economics of food production, its sustainability, the degree to which we waste food, how food production affects the natural environment and the impact of food on individual and population health (University of Oxford, 2018).

How are climate change and food systems connected?

Climate change and food systems are deeply intertwined. The way we cultivate, process, package, and transport food exerts significant pressure on the environment — driving greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, and pollution. At the same time, climate change is already disrupting the stability of food systems. Increasingly frequent and intense droughts, floods, and cold spells, along with the erosion of biodiversity, are placing global food security under growing threat (Fanzo & Miachon, 2023)

This cyclical relationship underscores the urgent need to reform our food systems. Adopting more sustainable and regenerative practices is not only essential for mitigating climate change but also for building resilience and ensuring a stable, secure food supply for future generations. The effects of climate change on human health do not just end with its impact on climate change, extreme weather events such as floods and heat stress will also affect the health and welfare of animals, which are a critical and important sources of nutrient dense food to humans.

Food Loss & Waste

Food loss and waste (FLW) is food that is never eaten for reasons such as spoilage, overproduction or simply tossing out perfectly good food. From production to consumption, FLW happens at every stage - at farms, stores and even in our homes. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), about 30% of all food produced is either lost or wasted. According to UNEP, the world already produces enough food to feed everyone on the planet and according to the recent Food Waste Index Report 2021, over 17% of food in total is wasted.

Around 38% of total energy consumption in the global food system is also utilized to produce food that is either lost or wasted (Geneva Environment Network, 2022). In addition, it takes seeds, soil and the labor of farmers to produce food, not to mention the fuel that is needed to transport it. All of these resources are lost when food is wasted. Furthermore, the disposal of food loss and waste in landfills leads to greenhouse gas emissions, contributing to climate change. Policymakers can improve the chances of achieving climate goals and limiting global warming to 1.5°C by making more specific commitments to transforming national food systems.

Pollution

Modern agricultural practices heavily depend on synthetic substances to boost productivity and cut production costs. Chemical fertilizers, pesticides, antibiotics, and plastic materials used in farming, livestock production, fishing, and aquaculture are significant contributors to water and soil pollution. The release of these pollutants into the environment poses serious risks not only to ecosystems but also to human health. Harmful substances include pathogens from livestock, pesticides, nitrates in groundwater, trace metals, toxic chemicals, and antibiotic-resistant genes released through animal waste.

Deforestation and Land Use Change

Deforestation and agricultural expansion represent one of the most critical environmental challenges of our time. Agriculture drives almost 90% of global deforestation, with commercial farming being the primary driver in tropical regions (UNFAO, 2021). The conversion of forests to agricultural land occurs through several pathways: Large-scale commercial agriculture (primarily cattle ranching, soy cultivation, and palm oil plantations) Small-scale subsistence farming Infrastructure development to support food production and distribution

Loss of Biodiversity

Food systems are both a driver of environmental change and a key factor in biodiversity loss. Agriculture, especially animal farming, contributes significantly to deforestation, habitat destruction, and species extinction. Agriculture uses half of the Earth's habitable land and has caused about 90% of global deforestation from 2000 to 2018 (FAO, 2021). Since the advent of agriculture, about a third of the planet's forests have been lost, with rates accelerating in recent centuries. Agricultural expansion, particularly for livestock, directly impacts forests, wetlands, and grasslands, leading to habitat loss and extinction of species. Currently, agriculture threatens over 25,000 species (Daniel Braune, 2024) , and by 2050, 88% of 20,000 terrestrial vertebrates will lose part of their habitat to agriculture, with 1,300 losing over 25% (Williams et al., 2020).

What We Can Do

Regenerative Farming

Regenerative agriculture focuses on revitalizing degraded soils through ecological management practices such as adaptive grazing, no-till planting, and minimal or no use of pesticides and synthetic fertilizers. Rather than simply sustaining the land, regenerative agriculture aims to collaborate with nature to restore and enhance soil health, reversing degradation and improving its overall vitality.tain the health of the soil, enhance biodiversity, and ensure the long-term sustainability of agricultural practices (Research Institute, 2024).

Supporting Smallholder Farmers

Supporting smallholder farmers with access to better resources, education, and financial assistance allows them to transition to more sustainable farming methods.

Empowering smallholder farmers contributes to food sovereignty, promotes biodiversity, and supports rural economies, while often reducing environmental impacts.

Vertical Farming & Urban Agriculture

Vertical farming grows crops in stacked layers using hydroponics, aeroponics, or aquaponics, minimizing land and water use. Urban agriculture utilizes rooftops, vacant lots, and greenhouses to grow food within cities.

These methods reduce transportation emissions, use resources efficiently, and improve food security by providing fresh, local produce year-round. They also promote community engagement, greener cities, and climate resilience. With advancements in AI and sustainable energy, urban and vertical farming are shaping the future of sustainable food production (Alex Tabibi, 2024).
